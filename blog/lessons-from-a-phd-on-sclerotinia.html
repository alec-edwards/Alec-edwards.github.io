From the lab bench to the trial plot: lessons from a PhD on Sclerotinia stem rot

Sclerotinia stem rot can strip a soybean field in a cool, wet season and leave it untouched the next. Trying to manage it with salicylic acid and native microbes taught me most of what I now know about doing research that farmers can use.

Sclerotinia sclerotiorum is one of the least fussy pathogens in agriculture: it infects hundreds of plant species, and its hardened resting bodies, the sclerotia, can survive in soil for years. In soybean it causes Sclerotinia stem rot, a disease that strikes around flowering when conditions are cool and moist. There are few resistant cultivars and fungicide timing is difficult, which makes it a natural target for biological approaches.

My MSc and PhD at the University of the Free State both tackled it, first with salicylic acid, a plant defence hormone, and then with plant growth-promoting bacteria and native microbial consortia isolated from soybean and sunflower roots. Headline result: include your 47–63% reduction in disease severity, or wait for publication?

Here are the lessons that have stayed with me.

1. Let the literature vote before you start

Before isolating a single microbe, I ran a meta-analysis of published work on plant growth-promoting bacteria. Pooling studies quantitatively shows which approaches deliver consistently and which rest on a few striking results. It is the same logic I now apply to biostimulant trials: look at the whole body of evidence, not the best headline.

2. What works in a Petri dish often fails in a field

Plenty of bacteria stop a fungus growing on agar. Far fewer survive in a real root zone, compete with resident microbes and still protect the plant. Screening in the lab is necessary, but it is only the first filter. The field is the test that matters.

3. Communities can beat champions

Single "super strains" are attractive but fragile. Working with consortia of native microbes, isolated from the crops and soils where they would be used, was a deliberate move towards something more likely to establish and persist.

4. Plan for seasons that don't cooperate

Because Sclerotinia depends on the weather, a dry season can produce almost no disease, and therefore no way to tell whether a treatment works. Running field validation over two seasons was essential. It is why I now insist on multi-season, multi-site data before drawing conclusions.

5. Measure the plant and its microbes, not just the symptoms

Disease scores tell you what happened; quantitative PCR of pathogen load and profiles of the root microbiome help explain why. Measuring at several levels, from field to molecule, is now the backbone of how I design trials.

6. Learn to explain it in three minutes

Competing in the Three Minute Thesis forced me to strip the project down to what mattered and why anyone should care. That skill, more than any single technique, has carried over into working with growers, distributors and commercial teams.
