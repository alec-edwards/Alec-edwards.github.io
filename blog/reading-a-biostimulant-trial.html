Formulation or function? How to read a biostimulant trial

Biostimulant trial results range from multi-season replicated studies to a single strip beside the farm gate. Here are the seven questions I ask before I believe any of them, including our own.

A plant biostimulant is defined by what it does, not by what it contains. Under the EU Fertilising Products Regulation, a plant biostimulant stimulates plant nutrition processes independently of its own nutrient content, with the aim of improving nutrient use efficiency, tolerance to abiotic stress, quality traits, or the availability of nutrients confined in the soil or root zone. That definition puts the burden of proof on the trial. If the claim is about function, the trial has to show function.

These are the questions I work through, in roughly this order.

1. What was it compared with?

An untreated control is the minimum. But many biostimulants, seaweed extracts included, also carry small amounts of nutrients, hormones or carbohydrates. If a product beats the untreated plots, was that the biostimulant effect, or just the extra nitrogen and potassium? A nutrient-matched control, or a comparison with a product of similar composition, is what separates formulation from function.

2. Was it replicated and randomised?

One treated strip next to one untreated strip tells you very little, because fields vary more than most treatment effects. Look for randomised complete block designs with at least four replicates, so that soil and slope differences are spread across treatments rather than confounded with them.

3. Is the replicate really a replicate?

Twenty plants sampled from one plot are twenty subsamples, not twenty replicates. Analysing them as if they were independent, known as pseudoreplication, shrinks the error term and makes almost anything look significant. The experimental unit is the thing the treatment was randomly applied to, usually the plot.

4. How big is the effect, and how sure are we?

A p-value tells you how surprising the data would be if the product did nothing. It does not tell you how much it did. I want the effect size with a confidence interval: a 4% yield increase with an interval from 1% to 7% is a very different business case from 4% with an interval from −6% to 14%. Statistically significant and agronomically worthwhile are separate questions.

5. Did it work under conditions like yours?

Biostimulant responses are often reported to be larger under stress, such as drought, heat, salinity or low nutrient supply, than in well-managed, unstressed crops. A trial in a perfect season may understate the effect; a trial under severe stress may overstate it for a typical year. The trial report should say what the growing conditions were.

6. Was it repeated?

A single significant trial can be a fluke; a single non-significant one can be bad luck. What persuades me is the pattern across many trials: how often the product came out ahead (a win rate, tested with a sign test), and the pooled effect size across all of them, including the trials that did not work.

7. Who ran it, and what was reported?

Trials run by independent contract research organisations or universities, to a protocol written before the season, carry more weight. So does a report that includes every variable measured, not only the ones that moved. If only the good news is in the brochure, ask for the full dataset.

A short checklist

  • Untreated and, ideally, nutrient-matched controls
  • Randomised, blocked design with at least four replicates
  • Analysis at the level of the plot, not the plant
  • Effect sizes with confidence intervals
  • Growing conditions described, including any stress
  • Results across seasons and sites, negatives included
  • An independent operator and a pre-agreed protocol

None of this is specific to seaweed or to any one company. It is how I would want any product, including the ones I work on, to be judged.
