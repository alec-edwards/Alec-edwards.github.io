What soil microbes reveal about the health of mountain soils

High on the Amphitheatre summit in the northern Drakensberg, the soils store carbon and feed the headwaters of the Thukela River. We set out to measure how alive they are, so that future change can be detected.

Alpine soils in the northern Maloti–Drakensberg do quiet but vital work: they hold large amounts of carbon, support unique plant communities and regulate water that flows to millions of people downstream. Grazing and tourism put them under pressure, yet very little was known about the microbial life that keeps them functioning.

Our study, led by Cowan Mc Lean at the University of the Free State, is the first coordinated measurement of the microbial and physical-chemical properties of these summit soils. Add a sentence on your own contribution to the study

What we measured

We sampled 30 topsoils on the Amphitheatre summit and measured their physical properties (texture, bulk density, aggregate stability), chemistry (pH, organic carbon, total carbon and nitrogen, and active carbon), and biology: overall microbial activity and community-level physiological profiles, which describe what the microbial community can break down rather than which species are present.

What we found

The soils were acidic (pH around 4.9), sandy loams with a low bulk density of about 1.01 g/cm³ and very high organic carbon, close to 10%. Organic carbon, active carbon, total nitrogen and microbial activity all rose and fell together, tying the soil's organic matter directly to how active its microbes are.

The microbial profiles split the samples into three functional groups. What separated them was mainly organic carbon, active carbon and microbial activity, not the type of soil or its physical properties.

Why it matters

Soil organic carbon and total nitrogen turned out to be the most useful indicators of how well the microbial community is functioning in these soils. Both are relatively cheap to measure, which makes long-term monitoring practical. The study gives a baseline: if grazing, trampling or a warming climate begin to degrade these soils, future sampling can detect it against these numbers.

For land managers, the message is simple. Protecting soil organic matter, through careful grazing and tourism management, protects the living engine of these high-altitude soils.
